### 2.1 Architecture of Multimodal Large Language Models

The architecture of multimodal large language models (LLMs) has evolved significantly over the years, enabling them to process and generate text, images, and other forms of data. This evolution is a crucial aspect of their development, as it allows them to learn from and interact with complex environments. In the design of multimodal LLMs, the architecture plays a crucial role in enabling them to integrate multiple modalities, learn from and interact with complex environments, and use various architectural components, training objectives, and evaluation metrics [1].

One of the key components of multimodal LLMs is the encoder-decoder architecture [2; 3]. The encoder takes in the input data, which can be text, images, or a combination of both, and converts it into a meaningful representation. This representation is then passed through the decoder, which generates the output based on the input data [4]. The encoder-decoder architecture has been widely used in various applications, including machine translation and text summarization.

The use of attention mechanisms in multimodal LLMs is another important aspect of their design [5]. The attention mechanism allows the model to focus on specific parts of the input data that are relevant to the task at hand. This is particularly useful in tasks such as machine translation, where the model needs to focus on specific words or phrases in the source language to generate the correct translation [6].

In addition to the encoder-decoder architecture and attention mechanism, multimodal LLMs also use various techniques to handle the fusion of different modalities. One such technique is the late fusion method [7], which involves combining the outputs of multiple models trained on different modalities. Another technique is the early fusion method [8], which involves combining the input data from multiple modalities before passing it through the model [9].

The architecture of multimodal LLMs has been influenced by the development of new hardware and software technologies, which have enabled the processing and generation of text, images, and other forms of data. For example, the use of convolutional neural networks (CNNs) [10], recurrent neural networks (RNNs) [11], and transformers [5] has led to significant improvements in various applications.

The use of multimodal LLMs has led to significant improvements in various applications, including language translation, text summarization, and image generation. For example, the use of multimodal LLMs has led to improvements in machine translation, where the model can generate more accurate and fluent translations [2]. Similarly, the use of multimodal LLMs has led to improvements in text summarization, where the model can generate more accurate and informative summaries [3].

## 3 Reinforcement Learning Fundamentals

### 3.1 Basics of Reinforcement Learning

Reinforcement learning is a subfield of machine learning that deals with training agents to make decisions in complex, uncertain environments. The emergence of reinforcement learning environments, such as simulation, real-world, and hybrid environments, has enabled agents to learn from their interactions with the environment and improve their performance over time.

The concept of reinforcement learning was first introduced by Richard Sutton in 1996, who defined it as a framework for learning how to map situations to actions in a way that maximizes a cumulative reward signal [25]. This framework has been further developed and extended to include various types of reinforcement learning, such as model-based, model-free, deep, and evolutionary reinforcement learning.

The basic components of reinforcement learning include the agent, the environment, the policy, and the reward function. The agent is the decision-making entity that interacts with the environment and receives the reward signal. The environment is the external world that the agent interacts with, and it can be a physical system, a computer program, or a combination of both. The policy is the mapping from the state of the environment to the action that the agent takes. The reward function is a function that evaluates the agent's actions and provides a feedback signal to the agent.

In the context of reinforcement learning environments, the agent's ability to learn from its interactions with the environment is crucial. Simulation environments, such as those discussed in the previous section, provide a controlled and safe environment for agents to learn from. Real-world environments, such as those discussed in the following section, provide a more challenging and dynamic environment for agents to learn from. Hybrid environments, which combine elements of both simulation and real-world environments, provide a more realistic and challenging environment for agents to learn from.

To address the challenges of reinforcement learning, researchers have developed a range of techniques, including Q-learning, SARSA, Deep Q-Networks, and Policy Gradient Methods. These algorithms have been successful in a wide range of applications, including game playing, robotics, and autonomous systems [26]. However, there are still many challenges to be addressed, including the need for more efficient algorithms, more effective exploration strategies, and more robust learning methods [27].

The emergence of large language models (LLMs) has also opened up new possibilities for reinforcement learning. LLMs can be used to generate policies, value functions, and other components of the reinforcement learning framework. LLMs have the potential to revolutionize the field of reinforcement learning by enabling agents to learn from large amounts of data and to generate policies that are more efficient and effective. However, there are also challenges to be addressed, including the need to scale LLMs to handle complex environments and to develop more efficient learning methods [28].

Overall, reinforcement learning is a rich and active area of research that has the potential to revolutionize the way we interact with complex environments. The development of reinforcement learning environments and the emergence of LLMs have enabled agents to learn from their interactions with the environment and to improve their performance over time. Reinforcement learning environments play a crucial role in training and evaluating reinforcement learning agents. These environments can be broadly classified into three categories: simulation environments, real-world environments, and hybrid environments.

Simulation environments, such as those used in robotics and autonomous driving, provide a controlled and safe environment for agents to learn from [29; 30]. These environments can be further divided into two subcategories: physics-based simulations and graph-based simulations. Physics-based simulations, such as those used in robotics and autonomous driving, provide a realistic and dynamic environment for agents to learn from. In contrast, graph-based simulations, such as those used in games and financial markets, provide a more abstract and structured environment for agents to learn from [31; 32]. Simulation environments have several advantages, including the ability to train agents in a controlled and safe manner, to reduce the cost of training, and to increase the speed of training. However, simulation environments also have several limitations, including the need for accurate modeling of real-world dynamics and the potential for overfitting to the simulated environment.

Real-world environments, on the other hand, provide a more challenging and dynamic environment for agents to learn from. These environments are often characterized by high levels of noise, uncertainty, and variability [33; 34]. Real-world environments can be further divided into two subcategories: static environments and dynamic environments. Static environments, such as those used in robotics and manufacturing, provide a relatively stable and predictable environment for agents to learn from [35]. In contrast, dynamic environments, such as those used in autonomous driving and finance, provide a more challenging and unpredictable environment for agents to learn from [36; 37]. Real-world environments have several advantages, including the ability to train agents in a more realistic and challenging manner, to increase the robustness and generalizability of agents, and to provide more accurate evaluation of agent performance. However, real-world environments also have several limitations, including the need for high-quality data collection and annotation, the potential for bias and corruption of data, and the need for careful consideration of safety and ethics.

Hybrid environments, which combine elements of both simulation and real-world environments, provide a more realistic and challenging environment for agents to learn from [38; 39]. Hybrid environments can be further divided into two subcategories: simulated-real environments and real-simulated environments. Simulated-real environments, such as those used in autonomous driving and finance, provide a simulated environment that is informed by real-world data and experience [40]. In contrast, real-simulated environments, such as those used in robotics and manufacturing, provide a real-world environment that is augmented by simulated components [41]. Hybrid environments have several advantages, including the ability to provide a more realistic and challenging environment for agents to learn from, to increase the robustness and generalizability of agents, and to provide more accurate evaluation of agent performance.

The emergence of large language models (LLMs) has also changed the landscape of reinforcement learning environments. LLMs can be used to generate realistic and diverse scenarios for simulation environments, while also providing a way to integrate real-world data and experience into the learning process [29; 30]. Additionally, LLMs can be used to create hybrid environments that combine elements of both simulation and real-world environments. The use of LLMs in reinforcement learning environments raises several research questions, including how to ensure the safety and ethics of LLM-based reinforcement learning systems, how to evaluate the performance and robustness of LLM-based reinforcement learning agents, and how to integrate LLMs into existing reinforcement learning frameworks.

In conclusion, reinforcement learning environments play a crucial role in training and evaluating reinforcement learning agents.
