\section{Method}

In this section, we describe our methodology to investigate the impact of different data distributions on the grokking phenomenon. We aim to systematically study how uniform, normal, and skewed distributions influence model performance by modifying dataset generation classes and conducting extensive experiments.

To generate datasets with different distributions, we extend the dataset generation classes (e.g., ModSumDataset, ModSubtractDataset, ModDivisonDataset, PermutationGroup) to include parameters for specifying the distribution type. The distributions considered in this study are:
- **Uniform distribution**: Each data point has an equal probability of being selected.
- **Normal distribution**: Data points are sampled from a Gaussian distribution centered around the mean.
- **Skewed distribution**: Data points follow an exponential distribution, resulting in a long tail.

The dataset classes handle these distributions by adjusting the sampling process. For instance, in the ModSumDataset class, we modify the index generation process to sample indices according to the specified distribution. This ensures that the generated datasets reflect the desired distribution characteristics. The same approach is applied to other dataset classes.

We update the data handling functions to accommodate the new distribution parameters. The 'operation_mod_p_data' function is modified to accept a distribution parameter and pass it to the dataset classes. This function generates the appropriate dataset based on the specified operation and distribution. Additionally, the 'get_data' function is updated to handle the new datasets and return data loaders for training and validation.

Our experimental setup involves training models on datasets with different distributions and evaluating their performance. We use a Transformer model \cite{vaswani2017attention} with two layers, a model dimension of 128, and four attention heads. The models are trained using the AdamW optimizer \cite{loshchilov2017adamw} with a learning rate of 1e-3 and a weight decay of 0.5. We use a learning rate scheduler with a warmup period of 50 steps. The primary metrics for evaluation are speed to grokking, final validation accuracy, and training stability.

During training, we log metrics such as training loss, validation loss, training accuracy, and validation accuracy. The training process is conducted for a total of 7500 updates, with evaluations performed every 10 updates. This allows us to monitor the model's performance over time and identify the point at which grokking occurs. The evaluation process involves computing the accuracy and loss on the validation set, providing insights into the model's generalization capabilities.

In summary, our method involves generating datasets with different distributions, updating data handling functions, and conducting extensive experiments to analyze the impact of these distributions on the grokking phenomenon. \label{sec:experimental}

We use synthetic datasets generated by the dataset classes described in the Method section. The datasets are split into training and validation sets with a training fraction of 0.7.

The primary evaluation metrics are speed to grokking, final validation accuracy, and training stability. Speed to grokking is measured as the number of training steps required to achieve a validation accuracy of 99\%. Final validation accuracy is the accuracy on the validation set at the end of training. Training stability is assessed by monitoring the variance in training loss and accuracy over time.

The models are trained using a Transformer architecture \cite{vaswani2017attention} with two layers, a model dimension of 128, and four attention heads. The AdamW optimizer \cite{loshchilov2017adamw} is used with a learning rate of 1e-3, betas of (0.9, 0.98), and a weight decay of 0.5. A learning rate scheduler with a warmup period of 50 steps is employed. The batch size is set to 512, and the total number of training updates is 7500.

The experiments are implemented using PyTorch \cite{paszke2019pytorch}. The models are trained on a single GPU, and the training process is logged to capture metrics such as training loss, validation loss, training accuracy, and validation accuracy. The datasets are generated on-the-fly during training to ensure that the data distribution characteristics are maintained.

In summary, our experimental setup involves generating synthetic datasets with different data distributions, training Transformer models on these datasets, and evaluating their performance using metrics such as speed to grokking, final validation accuracy, and training stability. This setup allows us to systematically study the impact of data distribution on the grokking phenomenon.
