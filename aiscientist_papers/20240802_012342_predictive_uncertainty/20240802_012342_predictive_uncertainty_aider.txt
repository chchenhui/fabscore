\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

% Overview of the Background section
In this section, we provide an overview of the key concepts and prior work that are essential for understanding our method. We also introduce the problem setting and notation used in our study.

% Paragraph 1: Introduction to Grokking
The grokking phenomenon, as described by \citet{power2022grokking}, refers to the unexpected generalization of neural networks after prolonged periods of overfitting. This phenomenon has been observed in small algorithmic datasets, where models initially overfit the training data but eventually achieve high validation accuracy after extended training. Understanding grokking is crucial for improving our knowledge of model behavior and training dynamics.

% Paragraph 2: Predictive Uncertainty
Predictive uncertainty quantification is a vital aspect of modern machine learning, particularly in safety-critical applications. Techniques such as Monte Carlo dropout \cite{gal2016dropout} and entropy measures are commonly used to estimate uncertainty during inference. These methods provide insights into the confidence of model predictions, which can be valuable for understanding and diagnosing model performance.

% Paragraph 3: Transformer Architecture
The Transformer architecture, introduced by \citet{vaswani2017attention}, has become a cornerstone in natural language processing and other domains. Its self-attention mechanism allows for efficient handling of long-range dependencies in sequential data. In our work, we modify the Transformer architecture to output both predicted classes and uncertainty measures, enabling a more comprehensive analysis of model behavior.

% Paragraph 4: Problem Setting and Notation
\subsection{Problem Setting}
\label{sec:problem_setting}
We focus on the problem of understanding the impact of predictive uncertainty on the grokking phenomenon. Specifically, we investigate this in the context of modular arithmetic and permutation groups. Let \( \mathcal{D} \) denote the dataset, which consists of input-output pairs \( (x, y) \). The goal is to train a model \( f_\theta \) parameterized by \( \theta \) to predict \( y \) from \( x \) while also quantifying the uncertainty of the predictions.

% Paragraph 5: Formalism
\subsection{Formalism}
\label{sec:formalism}
We denote the input space by \( \mathcal{X} \) and the output space by \( \mathcal{Y} \). The model \( f_\theta: \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y} \) is trained to minimize a loss function \( \mathcal{L}(f_\theta(x), y) \). To quantify predictive uncertainty, we use Monte Carlo dropout during inference, where multiple forward passes are performed with dropout enabled. The predictive mean \( \hat{y} \), variance \( \sigma^2 \), and entropy \( H \) are computed as follows:
\begin{align}
    \hat{y} &= \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} f_\theta^{(t)}(x), \\
    \sigma^2 &= \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} (f_\theta^{(t)}(x) - \hat{y})^2, \\
    H &= - \sum_{i=1}^{C} \hat{y}_i \log \hat{y}_i,
\end{align}
where \( T \) is the number of Monte Carlo samples, \( C \) is the number of classes, and \( f_\theta^{(t)}(x) \) denotes the \( t \)-th stochastic forward pass.

% Paragraph 6: Assumptions
\subsection{Assumptions}
\label{sec:assumptions}
