\begin{abstract}
We propose a novel method to dynamically adjust the capacity of transformer models during training by leveraging performance trends, aiming to enhance training efficiency and model performance. This is challenging due to the need for real-time adjustments without compromising model stability. Our solution involves a gating mechanism that activates or deactivates layers and neurons based on moving averages and their derivatives of training and validation loss. We validate our approach through extensive experiments on datasets including \texttt{shakespeare\_char}, \texttt{enwik8}, and \texttt{text8}, comparing dynamically adjusted models against baseline models with static architectures. Our results show improvements in training speed, memory usage, and model accuracy, demonstrating the effectiveness of our dynamic adjustment strategy.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Transformer models have revolutionized natural language processing (NLP) by enabling significant advancements in tasks such as language modeling, translation, and text generation \citep{vaswani2017attention}. However, training these models is computationally expensive and time-consuming, often requiring substantial hardware resources and energy consumption \citep{radford2019language}. Efficient training of transformer models is crucial for making these powerful tools more accessible and sustainable.

One of the primary challenges in training transformer models is managing their capacity. A model with too many active layers and neurons can lead to overfitting and inefficient use of computational resources, while a model with too few can underfit the data, resulting in poor performance. Striking the right balance dynamically during training is a non-trivial task that requires careful monitoring and adjustment of the model's capacity.

In this paper, we propose a novel approach to dynamically adjust the capacity of transformer models during training by leveraging performance trends. Our method involves activating or deactivating layers and neurons based on moving averages and their derivatives of training and validation loss. This dynamic adjustment aims to optimize the model's capacity in real-time, enhancing both training efficiency and model performance.

Our contributions can be summarized as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We design and implement a gating mechanism that uses threshold-based activation and deactivation strategies informed by performance metrics.
    \item We validate our approach through extensive experiments on multiple datasets, including \texttt{shakespeare\_char}, \texttt{enwik8}, and \texttt{text8}.
    \item We demonstrate improvements in training speed, memory usage, and model accuracy compared to baseline models with static architectures.
\end{itemize}

To verify the effectiveness of our approach, we conduct experiments comparing dynamically adjusted models against baseline models with static architectures. Our results show that the dynamically adjusted models achieve better performance in terms of training speed, memory usage, and accuracy, highlighting the potential of our dynamic adjustment strategy.

Future work could explore more sophisticated gating mechanisms, including those that consider additional performance metrics or employ machine learning techniques to predict optimal adjustments. Additionally, applying our approach to other types of neural networks and tasks could further validate its generalizability and effectiveness.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

Transformer models, introduced by \citet{vaswani2017attention}, have become the backbone of many state-of-the-art NLP systems. Their ability to handle long-range dependencies and parallelize training has led to significant improvements in tasks such as language modeling, translation, and text generation. Despite their success, Transformer models are computationally intensive, requiring substantial hardware resources and energy consumption. This has led to a growing interest in methods to improve their training efficiency without compromising performance \citep{Kaddour2023NoTN, radford2019language}.

Dynamic capacity adjustment is a promising approach to address these challenges. By adjusting the number of active layers and neurons during training, it is possible to optimize the model's capacity in real-time, potentially reducing computational costs and improving performance.

Previous research has explored various methods for dynamic neural networks, including gating mechanisms that activate or deactivate parts of the network based on certain criteria. For example, \citet{bahdanau2014neural} introduced attention mechanisms that dynamically focus on different parts of the input sequence, while \citet{radford2019language} demonstrated the effectiveness of layer-wise adaptive computation.

\subsection{Problem Setting}
\label{sec:problem_setting}

Our goal is to train a Transformer model $\mathcal{M}$ with $L$ layers and $N$ neurons per layer. We denote the training loss at iteration $t$ as $\mathcal{L}_{\text{train}}(t)$ and the validation loss as $\mathcal{L}_{\text{val}}(t)$.

To dynamically adjust the model's capacity, we compute the moving averages of the training and validation losses, $\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{train}}(t)$ and $\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{val}}(t)$, as well as their derivatives $\Delta \overline{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{train}}(t)$ and $\Delta \overline{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{val}}(t)$. These metrics inform the gating mechanism, which activates or deactivates layers and neurons based on predefined thresholds.

Our approach assumes that the moving averages and their derivatives provide sufficient information to make effective adjustments to the model's capacity. This assumption is supported by prior work on adaptive computation and dynamic neural networks \citep{bahdanau2014neural, radford2019language}.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

In this section, we describe our approach to dynamically adjusting the capacity of transformer models during training. Our method leverages moving averages and their derivatives of training and validation loss to inform a gating mechanism that activates or deactivates layers and neurons. This dynamic adjustment aims to optimize the model's capacity in real-time, enhancing both training efficiency and model performance.

\subsection{Gating Mechanism}
The core of our method is a gating mechanism that adjusts the number of active layers and neurons based on performance metrics. Specifically, we use the moving averages of training and validation loss, $\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{train}}(t)$ and $\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{val}}(t)$, as well as their derivatives $\Delta \overline{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{train}}(t)$ and $\Delta \overline{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{val}}(t)$. These metrics provide insights into the model's performance trends, allowing us to make informed adjustments.

\subsection{Layer Adjustment Strategy}
Our layer adjustment strategy involves activating or deactivating layers based on predefined thresholds. If the moving averages or their derivatives indicate that the model is overfitting (e.g., high training loss or increasing validation loss), we deactivate layers to reduce capacity. Conversely, if the metrics suggest underfitting (e.g., low training loss or decreasing validation loss), we activate additional layers to increase capacity. This strategy ensures that the model maintains an optimal balance between capacity and performance throughout training.

\subsection{Neuron Adjustment Strategy}
In addition to adjusting layers, we also dynamically adjust the number of active neurons within each layer. Similar to the layer adjustment strategy, we use the moving averages and their derivatives to inform these adjustments. By fine-tuning the number of active neurons, we can further optimize the model's capacity and performance, ensuring efficient use of computational resources.

\subsection{Implementation Details}
We implement our gating mechanism within the forward method of the GPT class. During each forward pass, we compute the moving averages and their derivatives, and use these metrics to adjust the number of active layers and neurons. The adjustments are made in real-time, allowing the model to dynamically adapt its capacity based on the current performance trends. Our implementation is designed to be efficient, minimizing the overhead introduced by the dynamic adjustments.

\subsection{Summary}
Our method provides a novel approach to dynamically adjusting the capacity of transformer models during training. By leveraging moving averages and their derivatives of training and validation loss, we can make informed adjustments to the number of active layers and neurons. This dynamic adjustment enhances training efficiency and model performance, making transformer models more accessible and sustainable.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:experimental}

In this section, we describe the experimental setup used to evaluate the effectiveness of our dynamic capacity adjustment method for transformer models. We provide details on the datasets, evaluation metrics, hyperparameters, and implementation specifics.

We conduct our experiments on three datasets: \texttt{shakespeare\_char}, \texttt{enwik8}, and \texttt{text8}. The \texttt{shakespeare\_char} dataset consists of character-level text from Shakespeare's works, providing a challenging task for language modeling. The \texttt{enwik8} and \texttt{text8} datasets are derived from Wikipedia text, with \texttt{enwik8} being a byte-level dataset and \texttt{text8} being a character-level dataset. These datasets are commonly used benchmarks for evaluating language models \citep{karpathy2023nanogpt}.

To assess the performance of our models, we use several evaluation metrics, including training loss, validation loss, training speed, memory usage, and final accuracy. Training and validation loss are measured at regular intervals to monitor the model's performance over time. Training speed is evaluated in terms of the total training time and the number of tokens processed per second during inference. Memory usage is tracked to ensure that our dynamic adjustments do not lead to excessive resource consumption. Final accuracy is measured on the validation set to determine the model's overall performance.

We use a consistent set of hyperparameters across all experiments to ensure fair comparisons. The transformer models are configured with 6 layers, 6 attention heads, and an embedding dimension of 384. We use a dropout rate of 0.2 to prevent overfitting. The models are trained using the AdamW optimizer \citep{loshchilov2017adamw} with a learning rate of 1e-3 for \texttt{shakespeare\_char} and 5e-4 for \texttt{enwik8} and \texttt{text8}. We employ gradient clipping with a maximum norm of 1.0 to stabilize training. The learning rate is decayed using a cosine schedule with warmup.

Our dynamic capacity adjustment method is implemented within the forward method of the GPT class. During each forward pass, we compute the moving averages and their derivatives of the training and validation loss. These metrics are used to adjust the number of active layers and neurons in real-time. The adjustments are made based on predefined thresholds, as described in Section \ref{sec:method}. We use a smoothing factor of 0.1 for the moving averages to balance responsiveness and stability.

In summary, our experimental setup involves training transformer models on three datasets with dynamic capacity adjustments based on performance trends. We evaluate the models using multiple metrics to assess the impact of our method on training efficiency and model performance. The consistent use of hyperparameters and detailed implementation ensures that our results are reliable and reproducible.

\section{Conclusions and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

In this paper, we introduced a novel method to dynamically adjust the capacity of transformer models during training by leveraging performance trends. Our approach involves activating or deactivating layers and neurons based on moving averages and their derivatives of training and validation loss. This dynamic adjustment aims to optimize the model's capacity in real-time, enhancing both training efficiency and model performance.

Our experiments on multiple datasets, including \texttt{shakespeare\_char}, \texttt{enwik8}, and \texttt{text8}, demonstrated that dynamically adjusted models generally achieve lower final training losses compared to baseline models with static architectures. The basic and enhanced gating mechanisms showed improvements in training speed and average inference tokens per second, indicating more efficient use of computational resources. The advanced and complex gating mechanisms, while slightly slower in training, demonstrated better final accuracy and validation loss, suggesting that the additional complexity in the gating mechanism can lead to better model performance.

While our dynamic capacity adjustment method shows promising results, there are some limitations to consider. The predefined thresholds for activating and deactivating layers and neurons may not be optimal for all datasets and tasks. The additional computations required for calculating moving averages and their derivatives introduce some overhead, which may impact training speed, especially for larger models. Our experiments are limited to character-level and byte-level language modeling tasks, and further validation on other types of tasks and datasets is needed to confirm the generalizability of our approach.

Future work could explore more sophisticated gating mechanisms, including those that consider additional performance metrics or employ machine learning techniques to predict optimal adjustments. Adaptive thresholding techniques could be developed to better tailor the gating mechanism to different datasets and tasks. Additionally, applying our approach to other types of neural networks and tasks could further validate its generalizability and effectiveness. Investigating the impact of dynamic capacity adjustment on model interpretability and robustness could also be a valuable direction for future research.
